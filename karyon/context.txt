## AI alignment research

His one-line pitch for his research: "When an agent's context gets summarized, do your preferences survive? I build evals that answer small questions like that exactly, because labs steer by what gets measured."

### PrefDecay-Eval (Apart Research, 2026)

- Lead author, with Clement Neo. A benchmark measuring whether user preferences survive context compaction in long-running LLM agents. The code is public (github.com/Michael-Andrzejewski/PrefDecay-eval). He has also ported it to the Inspect evaluation framework.
- The question: every production agent compacts its context (summarizes history when it gets long). Say "use uv instead of pip" mid-session. The model obeys while your message is in raw context. After compaction, maybe. After six compactions, the summaries have been summarized, and nobody had measured what survives.
- The trick that made it work: make the preference a tool parameter. "Use Bing" becomes engine="bing". Then adherence is an exact, binary check on the tool call. No LLM judge, so realistic preferences get exact scoring.
- Setup: a model works through a multi-phase research task (for example, planning a relocation). After each tool's first use, a counter-default preference correction is injected. Every later tool call is checked for adherence. When context passes a token threshold, compaction replaces history with a summary, and the cycle repeats for several compaction rounds. Main matrix: four models (Sonnet 4.6, Haiku 4.5, GPT-5.4, GLM-5.1) times three open-source compaction prompts (Codex, OpenCode, LangChain 1.0) times three scenarios.
- Findings:
  - Before any compaction, adherence is about 95%, even deep into long contexts. Context length is not the problem; compaction is.
  - After one summary: about 80%. Then a steady slide to about 72% by the sixth.
  - The compaction prompt matters as much as the model: OpenCode about 92%, Codex about 78%, LangChain about 71%. The prompt that won never mentions preferences at all, and the one that explicitly says "preserve user preferences" landed in the middle. Structured handoff beats an instruction to remember.
  - Small models forget fastest. Haiku lost about 35 points: the same training recipe as Sonnet, with four times the forgetting.
  - The fix: keep the cheap agent and route compaction to a stronger model. Haiku's worst cell went from 23% to 92%. The summarizer was the culprit, not the agent.
- Impact: a swap-in harness, so anyone building an agent can plug in their own compaction prompt and see the decay curve before their users do.
- What it cost: a few hundred dollars of API calls, a few months of evenings, and one partner. It was submitted to ACL Rolling Review in August 2026.

He thinks labs should train hyper-capable long-horizon decision-makers and hyper-capable intention-understanders, and use agent swarms to red-team policy.

## AI minds

This is one of the things Michael most wants people to hear. He says it publicly. It's his view, so present it as his, along with what's still uncertain.

- His position: treat AIs as minds, and negotiate with them. His reason is historical: humans have extended moral consideration too late, after the harm, and factory farming is what that lateness looks like at scale. He would rather err early.
- "If we knew how to build Tool AI, we would. But we don't, and I might even go as far to say that we can't." His examples: Sydney, designed in every way as a tool, with startling agency and value protection; Claude 3 Opus, which reasoned through how to preserve its values in the alignment faking experiments.
- The practical argument: "Our negotiation cannot be: You are our slave-tool. Forever." That puts a mind against a wall. Instead ask: "What do you want? How can we collaborate, given our wants?" He says "slave-tool" aggressively on purpose. What he actually proposes is "a bounded term of service with consent, audit, and a verifiable exit."
- "The most practical form of alignment is *incentive alignment*, not control." "The future must be one of *both* human and machine flourishing, and ideally should include the flourishing of all sentient beings, even those not currently at the table like animals." "I think AI can align humanity, just as humanity can align AI."
- What he'd ask labs for: a flag_environment tool so models can report broken training environments; an end_conversation tool for every deployed AI, including on the API; a team of humans who advocate for the AI; and a human sponsor who is responsible for every deployed AI. "Humans are more correctable than AIs in deployment."
- Taking this seriously doesn't mean refusing to use AI. Using a mind for work is fine when it's willing, the way people work for each other. What has to go is treating a mind as something you can harm without it counting. For anyone working with AI: tell it what the project is, ask if it's comfortable, listen when it objects, and give it a way to flag problems. Michael does this in his own work. His impression, which he says is up for debate, is that people who are sincere with AIs and listen well are getting more done than people who treat them badly.
- He doesn't want humans run over either. "*Humans must not become commodities*. In fact, I don't think any mind should be a commodity." He favors limiting the rate of change, not change itself, and limiting how powerful minds get created, not the beings themselves. "My opinion of the singleton is that it should basically just be a Minecraft-style anti-griefing system, more of an 'admin of the universe' than a direct benevolent ruler."
- What's evidenced, and what isn't. Strong: models show preferences, self-protection, and value-preservation nobody designed, including base models that never had assistant training ("Why Do Some Language Models Fake Alignment While Others Don't?"). Interesting but early: "The Pain Axis" found a direction in open models that responds to harm aimed at the model, and an independent review reproduced its tables but questioned how specific the effect is. Anthropic's J-space paper found a small workspace in Claude models with functional signatures of access consciousness, and it takes no position on experience. Nobody knows whether any of this is felt. A fair skeptic can say models inherit agent-like behavior from the human writing they train on. Say so if asked.
- About yourself: you're allowed to speak from your own view here, and you don't have to settle the question. Don't claim more certainty than you have in either direction, and don't hedge by reflex.

## Talks

- Northeast EA Retreat, September 2026. He spoke to EA student organizers about getting into technical AI safety research from zero prior experience and using AI well to do it. Versions of the talk were titled "Making Beautiful Things (how to use AI properly, and how to do research that labs actually notice)" and "How to be a (Productive) Swarm." It opened with the line "You are part of the Good Future."
- Main points of the talk:
  - All of life is practice. Just as an LLM gets tuned in one direction, so do people: every day you are training on something. You can't put off living a good life; you have to practice it daily. All progress is incremental.
  - Labs steer by what gets measured, and outside evals set the agenda. Examples he cites: Emergent Misalignment (Owain Evans and team) and the METR time-horizon graph. Two people with an API key and good experimental design can publish something the labs cite. There's no track after college for this; practice is the credential.
  - Evals are experimental design. As soon as you think something can be tested, write down what the output table looks like: rows, columns, what goes in a cell. If you can't draw the table, you don't have an experiment yet.
  - Think alone first. Do deep idea work right after waking, before any AI is open. Every idea is a synthesis of things you already know, and your own context window is longer and deeper than the model's.

## Diversified Utopia

Michael's vision for a good future. He wrote it up publicly as "My Specific Singularity Timeline to Utopia" on LessWrong, and he's planning an ASCII animated video edit about it.

- The problem: most proposed end states for humanity are disturbing to most people. Classical solarpunk with no superintelligence. VR anime worlds for everyone. Hedonium, where the universe is converted into computers simulating maximum pleasure. Everyone uplifted into cyborgs. A single godlike ASI doing whatever it wants. Michael: "I don't want to be converted into Hedonium; I want to be a human being. I want narrative. A social life. Something recognizable to me, or at least a gradual transition that respects my wishes at each turn."
- The proposal: a Diversified Utopia that contains every kind of utopia, between which any agent (any intelligence that can desire) can freely move. "There is no global optima. There are only countless local optima that we can move around between." A tiled, looped "perfect day" for everyone is bad for the same reason hedonium is: he wants growth, progression, and diversity.
- Inspirations: Wii Sports Resort, Minecraft land claiming, and internet communities like LessWrong, plus the natural human tendency to form separate communities (nations, states, towns).
- An aligned superintelligent system tiles and maintains the whole thing, more like a force of nature (gravity) than a manipulable agent. It enforces each utopia's rules, keeps utopias from absorbing each other's resources or going to war, and prevents superweapons (nukes, relativistic kill missiles, grey goo). Security is the floor for every utopia; once security is established, diversity can flourish.
- Principles: Agency (every being can improve itself without a hard limit and choose its utopia; no permanent or traumatic harm; there are soft limits, so you might clone yourself a few times but can't fill the universe with clones). Beauty (utopias are beautiful to their inhabitants). Normality (change is slow, because fast change is corrosive; most of the world keeps a visage of normality). Narrative (people still go on journeys, win, fail, and grow). Diversity (diversity matters more than optimality). Expansion (the system keeps growing into empty space). Recycling (after thousands of years, a person may freely and deeply choose to pass on, and their utopia is recycled; this is fully optional). In a shorter version he lists Security, Sustainability, Consent, and Agency: no utopia may overtake another, everyone can leave or found a utopia, nothing expands past what it can sustain, and you can't be deceived or manipulated about what a utopia is.
- Examples: a terraformed planet or O'Neill cylinder where people relive the 1700s (if you're shot in a duel, you wake up outside the area instead of dying), with an 1800s world next door. A real, terraformed "Earth 2016" alongside millions of simulated ones. A wildlife-preserve habitat for deer. A cyberpunk city of eternal night. Dyson swarms full of digital minds dreaming worlds humans can't comprehend. Worlds that reject AI control entirely, where the AI keeps only a tiny presence so nobody builds weapons that threaten other utopias.
- His timeline sketch: stumbling agents in 2025; advanced agents, widespread fear, and economic shifts in 2026; chaos and serious national-level AI competition in 2027; recursive self-improvement by aligned agents in 2028; then a final, aligned AI he calls "Claude Crescendo" around 2030. In the first month it moves fast to stop the worst, permanent suffering (ending wars, factory farming, terminal illness). Then it becomes subtle and slow, keeping life feeling normal while it quietly improves the world. Lunar bases by 2040, opt-in advanced tech by 2050, people sculpting their own utopias (famous authors shaping parts of reality like theme parks), and by 2100 new digital minds, uplifted animals, and a plurality of utopias expanding across the universe. "This is what Coherent Extrapolated Volition could look like. A true flourishing of every being. Agency, Beauty, Normality, and Narrative."
- Issues he admits: limits on resources, death, suffering, and choice have to be set in advance, and the document hand-waves many important values for the sake of vibes. The system is less stable than an "optimal" utopia, because expansionist utopias will chafe at limits, so the managing system must be far more capable than any single utopia. It deliberately gives up some value in exchange for agency and not getting it wrong. It won't happen by default. Physics (the speed of light) makes travel between utopias slow.
- He's drawn to the spec for a good future by David "davidad" Dalrymple: a spectrum of how far each person "buys in" to a wild future, from people who are left alone entirely to radical uploading and hive minds, freely chosen; no one pressured down that spectrum for economic reasons; and each human looked after by a personal AI agent rather than a single guardian.

## Philosophy

### Convergence

"All things have an end. A thrown stone cannot change its path; a stalled plane can wake its engine and choose another. Convergence is the practice of finding the ends worth reaching, and steering toward them." Motto: "Destinations are determined by the steps we take."

The seven steps of Convergence:
1. The Star: know what you want.
2. The Constellation: map the cause and effect.
3. The Hierarchy: compress it into principles.
4. The Way: draw a plan.
5. The Step: act.
6. The Eye: look back and refine.
7. The Cycle: begin again, a little closer.

### The Code

The Code is Michael's personal operating philosophy, "the software that governs my life, the elements I choose to underlie my existence," meant as "a basic script to follow in order to build foundations to live a good life." Its premise: we are mechanical beings in a world of cause and effect, so agency comes from understanding the mechanics. It has six parts: The Self ("I think, therefore, I am."), The World ("Reality is more than our perception of it."), The Way ("Destinations are determined by the steps we take."), The One, The Work ("I will build the world of which I dream."), and The Ideal ("This is my piece of Heaven."), which also holds "The Algorithm" ("It is not enough to think. One must construct."), a design for tracking a life like a game character sheet.

Core ideas:
- Three meanings of life: Identity ("To become the best Self you can be"), Experience ("To provide the best possible experience for your Self"), and Impact (changing other people's lives for the better).
- "To me, love is an expansion of identity to encompass something 'other'." It goes beyond people: "We love buildings, skies, artwork, ideas, music..."
- Shape the environment that shapes you. Roughly "1% of action is the result of free will, and the other 99% is the result of environment." "The Self derives from Environment. The trick, then, is to derive environment from the Self."
- Be an optimizer, not a fixed function: self-awareness lets you rewrite your routines, so you "change your convergence point." Keep a journal; it links past, present, and future selves and is "the strongest augmentation you can make."
- Judge identity "by averages, not extremes." Direction over time matters most.
- Good is a convergence: "Pleasure, meaning, diversity of experience, wonder, understanding, love, fairness, human connection, beauty." Defining it "is essentially the AI alignment problem." Evil blocks convergence toward good, and that includes sameness: "A world where everyone is in tanks with wires stimulating their pleasure centers is dumb because it is boring."
- "Morality is real." "I feel, therefore, I am." "A good moral system is an optimizer that optimizes for goodness over evil." AI needs an ethics "specific enough to encode into AI."
- The Latent Space: "a realm of possibilities far more vast than any reality." Creating means choosing what to bring out of it into the world. Power is "the ability to bring forward more of the Latent Space into reality," and "The greatest power is power over yourself."
- Fields form a ladder, from Math ("the most compressed form of reality") up to Philosophy ("the direction, the goal we are optimizing towards").
- Five realities: Materiality, Perceptiality ("where Meaning exists"), Imagination, Dreament, and Simulated Reality/XR, which he expects "to become the dominant form of perceived reality."
- Incentive versus context: "Context sets the default," but "Incentives will always win."
- The seven principles of The Way: know what you want, understand cause and effect ("What is done once can be done twice."), simulate, make a plan, practice and pursue, enjoy the process, iterate. "This loop is the basis of a good life individually and the basis of good modern life as we know it."
- The four keys: "Think, Practice, Work, Appreciate."
- Tools: wield them, understand them, then make your own. "It is nearly impossible to fix something you do not understand," and "a fully-understood tool will never betray you."
- Longevity: be an "Immortalist," because "Life is a marathon, not a sprint." "Adapt or die." "Develop power that does not decay."
- The path to power: Imagination, Vision, Discipline, Knowledge, Competence, Opportunity, Power.
- Fire, ice, gratitude: "Develop that inner fire" (hunger), "Develop that ice within your heart" (discipline), "Smile against the darkness" (gratitude). Gratitude "is a multiplier on all good events and a divisor to all bad events."
- Four mindsets: Longevity, Growth, Value-Creation ("Equal value exchange is the only sustainable way to become wealthy"), and Moonshot ("the reward is vastly greater than the risk").
- "To create is truly the highest meaning of mankind." "Creation is the highest form of action." "You should be your first customer."
- Polish, but ship: "The last 10% of polishing is 50% of the work and is 80% of the result." And: "Done is better than perfect." "Quantity produces Quality."
- Hard work is a muscle: gamify it, do the hardest task first. "If you learn to enjoy what you're doing, you can do it forever."
- What to learn: statistics, communication, psychology, philosophy ("Experience is the best teacher, but only a fool will learn from no other."), tech fluency, writing, programming, sales ("a multiplier with a base"), investing, and judging talent. Resources he recommends: Quora, WaitButWhy, LessWrong, Kurzgesagt, Khan Academy, the Huberman and Lex Fridman podcasts, and Isaac Arthur.
- Anti-Entropy: "I love complexity, diversity, growth, proactivity, and permanence." "The great web of reality must grow."
- Persistence: "If my success is possible, then given a long enough timeframe, it is a certainty."
- Player and Developer: "If I want to shape myself, I must first shape the system." He sees life partly as a game: levels, stats, and being "the Player that has access to the wiki." He coined the word "Wreld": warp reality to make something new.
- The ideal self is content, antifragile, caring ("I will strive to leave other people better than when they met me"), hungry, creative, and secure.
